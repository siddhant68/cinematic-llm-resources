## Why a correct script still sounds wrong

A script can pass every voice rule — no em dashes, no AI vocabulary, short
sentences, contractions everywhere — and still make a viewer feel lectured at.
The rules above are about *word choice*. The problem is *shape*.

Two shapes cause almost all of it:

1. **Uniform beats.** Every line roughly the same length, each on its own line
   with a blank line around it. The performer pauses after every one. Forty
   minutes of evenly spaced pronouncements.
2. **A closed loop per idea.** Claim, illustration, resolution, next claim. No
   arrival, no aside, no acknowledgment that a person is listening. Each unit is
   complete, so nothing pulls forward and nothing feels shared.

Both come from over-applying good advice. "Write short sentences" is a *floor*,
not a target. "Every section makes a claim" is about avoiding throat-clearing,
not about severing sections from each other.

## The beat-length budget

A "beat" is one spoken paragraph in the script: the unit between blank lines,
which is the unit the performer treats as a breath.

| Metric | Target | Failure symptom |
|---|---|---|
| Median words per beat | **12-18** | At 6, the read becomes a litany |
| Beats under 8 words | **under 35%** | Above 60%, every line sounds like a punchline |
| Longest beat | 30-40 words | Nothing over ~40; that is where a breath actually runs out |
| Beats over 20 words | at least 15% | Zero long beats means no momentum anywhere |

The one-breath rule still holds. A beat of 25 words is one breath at a normal
speaking pace; a beat of 6 words is a quarter of one, and stacking a hundred of
them is what produces the teleprompter effect.

**How to fix a litany.** Find three consecutive short beats and ask which one is
the point. Join the other two onto it as clauses, and let the point stand alone.

Before, six beats, all equal weight:

```
A wide shot gives us the world.

A medium brings us into the interaction.

A close-up gives us the emotion.

A detail tells us what suddenly matters.

Then you pull back.

And show the consequence.
```

After, three beats, one of them carrying the weight:

```
Wide gives you the world, medium puts you in the interaction, close-up gives
you the emotion, and a detail tells you what suddenly matters.

Most people stop there. They keep pushing in, tighter and tighter, because
tighter feels more dramatic.

Then pull back. The wide shot after the close-up is the one that hurts.
```

Same content, roughly the same word count, and now it has a floor, a turn and a
landing instead of six flat statements.

**Rhythm is variation, not length.** Two long beats then a four-word one lands
hard. Six four-word beats land nothing.

## Connective tissue

The lines between sections and between ideas. They cost a few seconds each and
they are most of what separates a conversation from a document.

Kinds worth using:

| Kind | Example |
|---|---|
| **Arrival** | "That was the easy half." |
| **Escalation** | "Same problem, except now there are two people in the frame." |
| **Admission** | "I got this backwards for about a month." |
| **Anticipation** | "You are probably already seeing where this breaks." |
| **Aside** | "This one is boring and it matters more than the rest of them." |
| **Correction** | "Which is not quite true, and I will come back to it." |

Two rules:

- **Arrive, then claim.** Connective tissue is one line, not a recap. "That was
  the easy half" is arrival. "So far we have covered shot size, camera position
  and framing, and now we will look at blocking" is a table of contents read
  aloud, which is dead air.
- **The claim still has to be a claim.** Connection is not permission to open a
  section with context. It buys one line of transition, then the section starts.

## Questions

Rhetorical questions are the default move in explanatory writing and the fastest
way to sound like a lecture, because in a lecture the asker always answers.

Three ways to use one, in descending order of how conversational they feel:

1. **Ask and leave it.** "So which one of those is wrong?" Then move to the
   demonstration and let the viewer answer it themselves before you confirm.
   Genuinely conversational, and it functions as a small open loop.
2. **Ask and answer against expectation.** "So the fix is a better prompt, right?
   No. The fix was deleting half of them."
3. **Ask and answer directly.** "How much of the scene should we see? A wide shot
   gives us the world." Fine occasionally. It is the one that becomes lecture
   cadence when it is the only mode in the script.

**The test:** count questions, and count how many are answered within two beats.
If nearly all of them are, convert a third to types 1 and 2. A script with
thirty self-answered questions has asked the viewer nothing.

Never ask a question the viewer cannot possibly have an opinion about. "What is
blocking?" is not a question, it is a heading spoken aloud.

## The register of a person

**Second person, singular.** "You" is one viewer. "We" is a textbook, unless the
we is genuine — you and the viewer looking at the same frame ("watch what happens
when we cut here") is fine; "now we need to decide where the camera goes" is a
manual.

**First person, specific.** Not "one might find" and not "people often". "I spent
two weeks on this." "I still get this wrong." The specificity is the credibility.

**Say the unflattering version.** "This sounds obvious when I say it slowly. It
was not obvious to me for a month." An admission does more for trust than any
credential, and it makes the correction memorable because the viewer now has a
story attached to it.

**Let some sentences be unimpressive.** A script where every line is quotable is
exhausting and reads as written. Ordinary connecting sentences give the good ones
somewhere to stand out from.

**Assert, then qualify once.** "Warm light makes the room feel welcoming" beats
"warm light can often make the same room feel somewhat more welcoming". Stacked
modals (can, may, might, sometimes, often) are usually a decision that has not
been made. Make it, then add the one real exception if there is one.

## Repetition that survives a vocabulary check

The repetition that makes a long video tiring is rarely verbatim. Four kinds, all
invisible to a word-frequency check:

**1. The repeated rhetorical move.** One sentence shape used as the default. The
common offenders:

- Antithesis: "X isn't Y, it's Z." Strong once. By the fifth section it is a tic.
- Concession: "It looks beautiful, but it fails." If every section is this, the
  episode has one argument stated eight times.
- The triad: three parallel items. Generated text reaches for three constantly.

Budget: any single shape opens at most about a fifth of the sections.

**2. The re-listed set.** A named set of concepts read aloud in full more than
once. A list of eight items takes twenty seconds and teaches nothing on the
second pass, because a viewer who did not retain it the first time will not
retain it now.

Read the list **once**, in full, where it is introduced. Everywhere else, refer
to it: "the eight decisions", "the third one", "the one everybody skips".

The closing section is where this bites hardest. A checklist recap followed by a
list recap is the same content twice, at the moment the most committed viewers
are still watching. Pick one, and make it the one that asks something of them.

**3. The re-established premise.** When sections cannot reference each other,
each one re-explains why the topic matters. Three sentences per section, twelve
sections, four minutes of the same premise. The fix is structural: let sections
connect (see Connective tissue) so the premise is established once.

**4. The duplicated claim.** The same insight arriving in three sections in
different words, because it was genuinely relevant in all three. This one is the
hardest to see while writing and the reason for the claim ledger.

## The claim ledger

A table built after the draft exists, before the polish pass.

| Claim | Home section | Also appears in | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beautiful frames can fail as shots | 03 SHOT | 05, 07, 08, 09, 10 | Say once at full strength in 03; elsewhere cut to a clause or drop |
| Light needs a visible source | 08 LIGHT | 12 | Keep 08, cut 12 to a callback |
| Continuity is state, not appearance | 10 CONTINUITY | — | Fine |

How to use it:

1. List every distinct claim the script makes. Not sections — claims.
2. Give each exactly one home: the section where it is best *demonstrated*, not
   where it first appears.
3. Everywhere else, cut it to a half-sentence callback or delete it.
4. If a claim has five homes, the episode has one argument and five illustrations
   of it. That is a fine episode, but it needs to be stated once at full strength
   and then the sections need to show different *consequences*, not restate it.

The ledger usually removes two to four minutes from a forty-minute script and
makes the remaining runtime feel faster.

## Making an idea easy to grasp

**Anchor abstractions to something on screen.** Three abstract claims in a row
teach less than one claim attached to a shot the viewer is looking at. If the
episode has its own footage, use it — the same one or two examples across every
section, so the viewer watches one thing get better instead of meeting eight
strangers.

**One new idea per beat.** If a beat introduces a concept and its exception at
once, split it. The exception is a separate beat, or it is cut.

**Concrete before abstract, always.** Show the failure, then name it. "The prop
was on the table, and in the next shot it is on the floor. That is a state
continuity break" lands. "State continuity breaks occur when object properties
fail to persist across generation boundaries" does not.

**Name things once and reuse the name.** If a concept is called "environment
blocking", it is called that every time. Synonym rotation ("spatial planning",
"stage geometry", "world layout") reads as varied writing and costs the viewer a
lookup every time.

**Cut the definition of anything the footage shows.** If the screen shows a high
angle and a low angle, the viewer has learned high and low angles. Twenty more
seconds explaining them is where the retention graph sags.

## Self-audit

Before the voice pass, on the finished draft:

- [ ] Median beat length is 12-18 words; under 35% of beats are under 8 words
- [ ] At least one connective line arriving into most sections
- [ ] No section opener is a recap of the previous section
- [ ] Under a fifth of sections open with the same sentence shape
- [ ] Every named set is read aloud in full exactly once
- [ ] Claim ledger built; no claim has more than one home
- [ ] The close has one recap, not two
- [ ] At least a few questions are left for the viewer to answer
- [ ] At least one genuine admission or correction in the first third
- [ ] Every abstract claim is anchored to something visible
- [ ] Read the first two minutes and the turn out loud. If you stumble, rewrite

`scripts/scriptcheck.py --cadence` reports the measurable ones. The rest are
judgment.
