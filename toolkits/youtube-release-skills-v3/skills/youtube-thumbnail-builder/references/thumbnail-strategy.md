## 1. Promise alignment

A thumbnail is a visual half of one promise. The title supplies precision or curiosity; the image supplies proof, tension, scale, or emotion. Do not repeat the exact same words and visual meaning in both.

For every candidate, write one sentence:

> A viewer should click because they understand that this video will help them [outcome], and the image proves it by showing [evidence].

If that sentence cannot be written without exaggeration, redesign the concept.

## 2. Concept families

### Outcome/proof

Best when the finished artifact is visually compelling. Show one clear result and a restrained comparison when useful.

### Problem/tension

Best when the audience already feels a pain. Show the failure state, confusion, or tradeoff without manufacturing drama.

### System/process

Best when the workflow itself is differentiated. Simplify the system into one legible visual rather than a complete diagram.

### Identity/expertise

Use the creator's face only when trust, reaction, or personal experimentation is central. A face should not consume most of the frame merely because popular thumbnails do so.

## 3. Hierarchy

At a glance, the eye should move through no more than three levels:

1. dominant subject or result
2. short message, contrast, or directional cue
3. optional supporting proof

Blur or darken background detail when needed. Use separation, scale, lighting, and negative space before adding outlines and glow.

## 4. Faces, screenshots, and proof

- Select real frames with clean expression and minimal motion blur.
- Preserve believable proportions and skin texture.
- Crop interfaces to the one region that proves the claim.
- Enlarge small UI output instead of showing an unreadable full screen.
- When using before/after, make the axis and labels obvious.
- Do not insert output the tutorial never reaches.

## 5. Text

Use zero to four words by default. Text should add a second layer, not restate the title. Avoid generic labels such as “FULL GUIDE,” “INSANE,” or “YOU NEED THIS” unless the content truly needs that wording.

Use one type family, one main weight, and high contrast. Break lines manually. Keep punctuation minimal. Test at 160x90.

## 6. Candidate differentiation

Good variants change the underlying click hypothesis:

- search clarity versus browse curiosity
- final proof versus failure tension
- creator-led trust versus artifact-led proof

Weak variants change only:

- color
- border
- one synonym
- tiny expression differences
- left/right placement

## 7. Review checklist

- Is the promise true?
- Does the first minute begin paying it off?
- Can the idea be described in five seconds?
- Is the main subject obvious at 160x90?
- Does the thumbnail add information beyond the title?
- Is text readable without zooming?
- Are all assets owned, cleared, or generated under a compatible license?
- Does the image still work without saturation?
- Are face, hands, and interface details natural?
- Would a viewer feel misled after watching?
